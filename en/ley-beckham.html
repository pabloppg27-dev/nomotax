What exactly you gain

A flat rate instead of
a progressive scale

As a regular resident, your salary falls into a scale that rises bracket by bracket to almost 47%. Under the inbound workers regime you pay a flat rate of 24% and only on what you earn in Spain, except employment income, which is taxed worldwide.

24% On your salary Flat rate on the first 600.000 € of employment income.
47% Only on the excess Anything above 600.000 € is taxed at 47%. Below that, never.
6 Years it applies The year you move and the following five.
0 Form 720 filings While the regime lasts you don't report assets abroad or pay tax on your worldwide wealth.

I want the exact calculation for my case

Indicative estimate using the 2025 personal income tax scales. The 24% inbound workers rate is national and doesn't vary between regions. For the standard regime we use the default regional scale in art. 65 LIRPF as a middle reference: your region may raise or lower the bill by a few points. Social Security contributions up to the maximum base (58.914 €) and 2.000 € of other deductible expenses are subtracted, and the personal allowance is applied; under the inbound workers regime tax is paid on gross income with no reductions or personal allowance, as set out in art. 93 LIRPF. It does not include regional deductions, family circumstances, double tax treaties or tax already withheld. It does not replace a personalised study.

Who can apply

Four profiles qualify
for the regime

Moving isn't enough: the law requires the move to have a specific reason. These are the four ways in.

Employment contract

You are hired by a Spanish company, or transferred within your group. It also covers international remote work: you work remotely for a foreign company with the digital nomad visa. Professional athletes are excluded.

Company director

You come to Spain to manage a company. If it is an asset-holding entity, your stake must be below 25%; if it isn't, there is no percentage limit.

Entrepreneur

You start an activity in Spain that qualifies as entrepreneurial. It requires a favourable report from ENISA on the innovative nature of the project.

Highly qualified professional

You provide services to startups or carry out training, research, development and innovation. That income must account for more than 40% of your total earnings.

And in every case
  • 01 Not having been tax resident in Spain during the five tax periods before the move.
  • 02 The move must have a reason: one of the four above, documented.
  • 03 Not earning income through a permanent establishment in Spain, except for entrepreneurs and qualified professionals.

The regime extends to your spouse and children under 25 (or with a disability, of any age) if they move the same year or the next and their combined taxable bases are lower than yours. In practice, the whole family can benefit.

What is taxed and what isn't

You are taxed like a non-resident,
with one exception

You live in Spain but are taxed almost as if you didn't: only on your Spanish income. The exception is employment income, which is taxed wherever it comes from.

Income Taxed in Spain? Details
Salary, wherever it comes from Yes 24% up to 600.000 € and 47% on the excess. It is the only income taxed worldwide.
Spanish dividends and interest Yes Savings-income scale: 19% to 30%.
Foreign dividends and interest No Outside the Spanish tax base.
Gains from selling Spanish assets Yes Savings-income scale, like any non-resident.
Gains from selling foreign assets No This is the big advantage for anyone selling shares or portfolios abroad.
Wealth Tax Yes Only on a limited basis: assets and rights located in Spain. Not your worldwide wealth.
Form 720 (assets abroad) No You don't file it while the regime lasts, because Form 720 only applies to those taxed on their worldwide income. Note: in the first year you return to the standard regime you do have to file it, with everything you hold abroad at 31 December.

It doesn't always pay off

Under the inbound workers regime there is no personal allowance, no reductions and practically no deductions: you are taxed on the gross from the first euro. That's why, with salaries below roughly 55.000 € – 60.000 € the standard regime is usually cheaper. And if you have a lot of foreign-source income, the balance tips the other way. It's a decision about numbers, not headlines, and it's made only once.

Deadlines and filing

When to apply
and what starts the clock

This is where most people get it wrong. You don't apply when you arrive or when you register with the town hall: you apply from the start of your activity.

You have six months
from the start of your activity

The deadline to file Form 149 is six months from the start date of your activity. And it's worth spelling out what that means, because this is where almost everyone gets confused.

The start of activity is not the day you started working, nor the day you arrived. It is the date you are formally registered in Spain to carry out the activity that justifies your move: the one shown in your registration with Spanish Social Security. If you keep your home country's Social Security, it is the date shown in that documentation (form A1 or equivalent). And if you don't have to register anywhere, it is the start date of activity shown in your supporting documents.

In other words, the clock is started by a document with a date, not by your everyday life in Spain.

  • Registering with the town hall (padrón)
  • The day you land in Spain
  • Being granted the visa or NIE
  • Signing the employment contract
  • The start date of activity on your Social Security registration (or the document that replaces it)
The questions we get every week

I'm already living in Spain. Am I too late?

It depends on when you registered, not when you arrived. If six months haven't passed since the start date of your activity, you're in time. The first thing we do is check that date.

Should I have applied before coming?

No. Form 149 is filed after the move, not before. What does make sense is planning it beforehand, because the registration date and how you get paid shape the outcome.

I registered with the town hall months ago. Does it count?

Registering with the town hall has no tax effect on its own. It's a municipal register: it doesn't start the deadline, make you tax resident or end it.

I'm only here temporarily

If you spend more than 183 days in Spain in a calendar year, you are tax resident even if your stay is temporary. And then the comparison between the two regimes affects you fully.

I've been in Spain for a while, paid by my company abroad

The most common case: you live here, invoice or pay yourself from your company in another country and have been declaring it there. If you spend more than 183 days in Spain, you are already a Spanish tax resident, wherever you declare it. Your start of activity will be the date of the registration you use to regularise your situation here — but first the years you've already spent here need to be resolved.

Do those previous years disqualify me?

They can, and it's the first thing we check. The regime requires not having been tax resident in Spain in the previous five years. If you already were, even if you paid tax elsewhere, each of those years counts against you. That's why this situation is urgent: the sooner it's looked at, the more options there are.

I've already missed the deadline. Can anything be done?

The six months are a strict, non-extendable deadline: there's no extension or way to recover it. You'll be taxed under the standard regime. It's then worth reviewing what other optimisation routes you still have.

What if I opt in and later it doesn't suit me?

You can opt out in November and December, with effect from the following year. But once you opt out, you can't come back: that's why the calculation is done before, not after.
